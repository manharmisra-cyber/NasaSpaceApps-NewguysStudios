      Your lander

      Battery charge over the mission

      The mission starts at the selected time with a full battery and runs for the mission length chosen in the Timeline tab. The lander works while it is sunlit and drops to its dark-time power draw otherwise. Data is counted whenever Earth is in view, which assumes a ground station is always listening. The starting values are examples, not a real spacecraft. Hourly midpoint sampling, lossless battery, no charge/discharge limits or degradation; no radio link budget or station scheduling. These estimates do not certify mission feasibility.

      • The terrain is real. The imported dataset identifies its preset heights as NASA's LOLA laser altimeter (the 64 pixel-per-degree global grid, resampled to 500 m cells, 64 km across), plus a far-horizon profile from real terrain out to 400 km. Custom sites use the 16 pixel-per-degree grid (about 1.9 km cells, 243 km across). Features smaller than one cell are not in the data, so a real rim or boulder field closer than that can still block the Sun earlier than shown.
      • If elevation files fail to load, terrain-dependent results are withheld and a retry is offered. Smooth sphere and generated demo terrain are explicit alternatives. The demo is not a measured lunar location.
      • For every compass direction the tool walks outward across the height grid and records the highest angle the ground reaches, corrected for the Moon's curvature. The Sun or Earth counts as visible when it clears that profile. The large shadows in the 3D view come from the same test. Small craters, rocks and surface texture in the 3D view are decoration and are not part of the calculation.
      • Approximate positions. This view uses a truncated lunar theory and optical libration formulas from Meeus, Astronomical Algorithms. The separate JPL reference sky retains cached Horizons data for three fixed sites in November 2026. An arbitrary pin here is not a live Horizons query. See the packaged validation report for the bounded comparison.
      • The Moon's surface colour is NASA's LROC map and Earth is NASA's Blue Marble, both at 2048 x 1024, so they blur when you zoom far in. Earth is shown without clouds. Sizes, distances, phases, Earth's rotation and the tilt of both bodies are computed for the selected time.
      • The lander and rover are drawn at roughly real size, so zoom in to see them. The rover reports sunlight, Earth visibility and line of sight to the lander at its own position. Its lamps light the ground in the picture only, and its fast and ultra speeds are for getting around, not a claim about real rovers.
      • The flight follows a two-body transfer ellipse from a 200 km Earth orbit to 500 km above the Moon. It ignores the Moon's gravity and any engine burns after departure, so it shows the shape, speed and timing of the trip, not a flyable trajectory.
      • There is no atmosphere, so no refraction and no twilight. Ground temperature is a radiative-equilibrium estimate for flat sunlit regolith, with a fixed 95 K for unlit ground.
      • Site coordinates marked approx. are representative points. You can still load your own higher-resolution elevation tile (.asc) for a site in the Terrain and hardware panel.
